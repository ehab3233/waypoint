    What it accounts for

    The six things a naive optimizer gets wrong

    01

    Pin & lock

    Fix certain cities to certain positions — a flight already booked, a wedding on the 14th. Everything else reorders around the fixed points.

    02

    Backtracking allowed

    A naive loop optimizer refuses to produce Belgium→Germany→Belgium. Sometimes there-and-back genuinely beats the circle — Waypoint says so out loud.

    03

    Open-jaw awareness

    Fly into Amsterdam, out of Munich. Assuming a return trip quietly inflates every itinerary, so the return is priced separately and shown.

    04

    Rail pass math

    Does an Interrail pass beat point-to-point for this route, once seat reservations are counted? One line, one answer.

    05

    Budget carrier reality

    Ryanair and Wizz fly hub-to-hub, not city-to-city. A router assuming any two airports connect produces fiction.

    06

    Minimum stay

    Prevents the classic optimizer failure where you are “in Prague” for five hours at two in the morning.

    Positioning

    The layer above the booking sites — not a competitor to them.

    Waypoint decides the order and the mode, then hands you off to whoever sells the ticket.

    Surface corridors (rail, coach, ferry) and budget-carrier flights are curated typical values for concept validation, not live quotes. Longer air legs are modelled on a hub-and-spoke network — spokes feed their nearest hubs, hubs connect regionally, and only intercontinental gateways carry the long haul — so a connection costs real time and money instead of appearing as a fictional nonstop. Modelled fares are marked EST. Rail-pass math uses Interrail Global 2nd-class pricing.
